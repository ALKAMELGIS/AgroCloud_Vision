# Build the NDVI timeline before anything else

AgroCloud Vision should build a **per-field NDVI timeline first**. That means a date strip with per-field cloud %, an index map with a natural-colour check, a seasonal curve set against prior seasons, and rain/GDD sums from the sowing date. After that come NDVI-anomaly-driven scouting tasks, then multi-season productivity zones with SHP/ISO-XML VRA export, then soil sampling grids. Every leading platform puts the timeline on the field screen as soon as a field exists. Users and vendors value problem-area detection that tells you where to scout more than VRA, which serves a smaller, more advanced segment. For imagery, the zero-cost, zero-key path is **Element84 Earth Search v1 STAC (`sentinel-2-c1-l2a`) plus geotiff.js range reads of `red`, `nir` and `scl` COGs in a browser Web Worker**, which fits the static GitHub Pages front end. Behind it sits the **Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (CDSE) Statistical API** as the higher-quality fallback, called through a small NestJS token proxy: it masks clouds with SCL server-side and its free quota is 10,000 processing units a month. Planetary Computer is the route to Landsat 8/9, because Earth Search's Landsat is requester-pays. One caveat matters before committing: **CORS and HTTP Range behaviour on the Earth Search and Planetary Computer endpoints could not be verified live** during research, so a 10-minute browser smoke test is the first engineering task. The zone, sampling and anomaly algorithms vendors use are not published, so the methods below combine what vendors disclose, extension guidance and academic studies, with tunable defaults clearly marked as engineering choices.

## Six platforms converge on one field screen, then diverge on agronomy

Once a field is drawn, the two satellite-first tools, EOS Crop Monitoring and OneSoil, show an index map, a strip of image dates, a seasonal index chart, crop rotation and weather with accumulated precipitation and GDD. Zoning and VRA sit one click deeper. EOS defaults to **Sentinel-2 at 10 m**, with optional daily PlanetScope at 3 m. Its date line previews each image on hover with its **cloud and shadow percentage** and hides images above a configurable **50% cloud threshold**. It offers NDVI, NDRE, MSAVI, ReCI, NDMI and VMI built in, with a dozen more indices as add-ons. A natural-colour layer, a cloud/cirrus/shadow mask and split view let users check index artefacts, and a value-distribution panel shows the field in hectares or percent with XLS export ([EOS user guide: Fields](https://eos.com/products/crop-monitoring/user-guide/fields)). The EOS crop panel stores several crops per season with sowing dates and shows the current **BBCH stage**. Its weather chart marks cold stress at −6 °C and heat stress at +30 °C and switches to precipitation and root-zone moisture. The guide also lists Scouting, Field Leaderboard, Field Activity Log, Custom Report and a GDD (sum of active temperatures) view ([EOS user guide: Fields](https://eos.com/products/crop-monitoring/user-guide/fields)). AgroCloud's original mockup, with Monitoring, Leaderboard, Activity Log, VRA, Map Builder, Soil Sampling and Scout Tasks, closely mirrors this module list.

OneSoil's free web app compares NDVI and true-colour images across dates, charts NDVI over the season, predicts harvest dates from the NDVI curve, and charts accumulated precipitation and GDD. Its mobile scouting app adds a **"contrasted NDVI"** view that stretches values within the field, geotagged notes with photos, offline use and routing to each note ([OneSoil help](https://help.onesoil.ai/en/articles/5237584-how-the-onesoil-web-and-mobile-apps-differ)). OneSoil's 2026 releases show where the category is heading: **circle drawing for centre pivots** (Feb), VRA maps for DJI Agras drones (Jun), and a **per-field AI Agronomist** grounded in that field's zones, imagery history and logged operations (Jun) ([OneSoil latest releases](https://help.onesoil.ai/en/articles/11173083-latest-releases)).

The agribusiness platforms build on the same base and add agronomic models and machine connectivity. xarvio FIELD MANAGER offers "Powerzone" maps from **up to 15 years of satellite history**, seeding and N/P/K VRA with calculated rates, growth-stage and disease-risk alerts, spray-timing alerts, and wireless transfer to machine terminals ([xarvio](https://www.xarvio.com/us/en/products/field-manager.html)). Climate FieldView sells Field Health Imagery, image-enhanced scouting, Seed Scripts, Yield Analysis, field weather, SplitView and a Cab App ([FieldView pricing](https://climate.com/pricing)). Syngenta's Cropwise Explorer, announced in October 2026 for US retailers, centres on **anomaly detection plus AI-ranked weekly reports that tell agronomists which fields to scout first and why** ([Fertilizer Daily](https://www.fertilizerdaily.com/20261012-cropwise-explorer-syngenta-retailers/)). Its mobile predecessor already sent anomaly alerts by push, SMS and WhatsApp ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1547493499)). Bushel Farm, formerly FarmLogs, is the outlier. It leads with **per-field rainfall alerts compared against past seasons**, and puts satellite imagery only in its $599/yr Essentials tier ([Bushel Farm](https://www.bushelpowered.com/farmers/lite)).

Free tiers have narrowed in a consistent way. EOS gives free access to only the **last 3 months** of imagery ([EOS](https://eos.com/products/crop-monitoring/user-guide/fields)). Since May 2026, OneSoil's free mobile users see only the latest cloud-free image, notes and weather, while history, hyperlocal rain forecasts and spray windows are paid ([OneSoil freemium](https://onesoil.ai/en/resources/onesoil-mob-app-is-moving-to-freemium)). FieldView Basic is $0 but has no imagery, and Plus starts at **$649/yr** ([FieldView pricing](https://climate.com/pricing)). The pattern across vendors is that the latest image and notes are free, while history, zones/VRA, alerts and recommendations are paid. AgroCloud can copy that split.

Evidence on what users value is thin, with only a handful of reviews per product, but it points the same way. EOS reviewers call NDVI, NDMI and NDRE with history "very useful" and complain about revisit frequency and cloud gaps at key stages ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/195553/EOS-Crop-Monitoring/)). OneSoil users value its **identification of problem areas for scouting**, and reviewers note that VRA is unsuitable on homogeneous fields ([Future Farming](https://futurefarming.com/smart-farming/tools-data/onesoil-a-handy-free-fieldmap-and-scouting-app)). No usage data was found on rarely used features such as leaderboards or Map Builder.

### Recommended build order for AgroCloud Vision

AgroCloud already has farms, boundaries, crops per field, an activity log and a 7-day weather forecast, so it can skip the foundation and start at imagery. The order below follows the value signals above and the dependency chain: zones need history, anomalies need a reliable timeline, and sampling and P&K VRA need zones.

| # | Feature | Why now | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | NDVI timeline: date strip with per-field cloud %, NDVI map with absolute and "contrast" legend, natural-colour toggle, field mean/p10/p90 curve vs prior seasons | Universal baseline; most valued; free-tier hook | Imagery pipeline (next section) |
| 2 | Accumulated rain and GDD from sowing date, against past seasons | Cheap; extends existing weather + crops; Bushel's anchor feature | Crop sowing date, historical weather |
| 3 | Two-date compare (swipe/split) and NDVI anomaly detection → auto-suggested scouting tasks | "Where to scout first" is the 2026 differentiator | #1 |
| 4 | Scouting tasks: point/area on map, issue type, severity, photos, status, navigation, offline PWA | Turns imagery into action; links to the activity log | #3 (optional) |
| 5 | Productivity zones (multi-season NDVI) and VRA rates per zone with SHP + ISO-XML export and a flat-rate savings comparison | High value for advanced users; paid-tier anchor | Multi-season #1 |
| 6 | Soil sampling grids and zone-directed points, GPX/CSV export, lab CSV import, IDW P&K maps | Builds on zones; feeds P&K VRA | #5 |
| 7 | Field leaderboard / weekly digest, alerts, per-field AI assistant | Aggregates #1–#6; only credible once the data exists | All above |

A leaderboard is cheap to build once per-field NDVI statistics exist, but it ranks fields rather than telling users what to do. Cropwise's weekly digest of which fields to scout first is a more useful framing of the same data and is worth adopting instead.

## Earth Search COGs give keyless NDVI straight from the browser

Element84 Earth Search v1 (`https://earth-search.aws.element84.com/v1`) is a public STAC API. Its Sentinel-2 COGs sit in public `us-west-2` S3 buckets that need **no AWS account**, and new scenes usually arrive within a few hours of Copernicus publishing them ([AWS Open Data](https://registry.opendata.aws/sentinel-2-l2a-cogs/)). Use the `sentinel-2-c1-l2a` collection, ESA's Collection 1 reprocessing with a uniform baseline. Keep the collection ID configurable, because **Earth Search v2 is in beta and renames `sentinel-2-c1-l2a` to `sentinel-2-l2a`**. v0 is deprecated and slated for deletion ([Element84 GitHub](https://github.com/Element84/earth-search); [Element84 v1 blog](https://element84.com/geospatial/introducing-earth-search-v1-new-datasets-now-available)). Earth Search's **Landsat is requester-pays** and exposed only as `s3://` URLs, so a browser cannot read it ([Element84 v1 blog](https://element84.com/geospatial/introducing-earth-search-v1-new-datasets-now-available)). Neither the README nor the v1 announcement documents rate limits or CORS policy. Browser COG viewers commonly read these buckets directly, and a 2026 deck.gl demo streams Sentinel-2 COGs per tile in the browser, keeping a baked `items.json` fallback in case CORS changes ([sentinel-2-cog-deckgl-raster](https://tomevault.io/tome/kentstephen/sentinel-2-cog-deckgl-raster)).

The client-side pipeline is short. POST a STAC search with the field polygon as `intersects`, a date range and a loose `eo:cloud_cover` filter. That value covers the whole ~110 km tile and says little about a single field. For each candidate date, open the `red` (B04, 10 m), `nir` (B08, 10 m) and `scl` (20 m) assets with `GeoTIFF.fromUrl`. Project the field from WGS84 to the item's UTM zone with proj4 and read only the pixel window that covers the field. A 100 ha field is about 100×100 pixels, which means a few range requests and roughly 0.5–3 MB per date. That is an estimate, not a measurement. Upsample SCL by nearest neighbour, clip to the polygon, and compute mean, median, p10, p90 and the valid-pixel fraction in a Web Worker. Render NDVI to a canvas with an RdYlGn ramp and place it as a Leaflet `imageOverlay`. Cache date-to-stats results in IndexedDB and in PostGIS so no date is computed twice. Where a field straddles two MGRS tiles, choose the item that fully contains it and de-duplicate same-date items.

### Cloud masking and the offset that silently biases NDVI

Mask with the Scene Classification Layer. **Keep SCL 4 (vegetation) and 5 (bare soil)**, and optionally 6 and 7. **Drop 0, 1, 3 (cloud shadow), 8 and 9 (medium/high-probability cloud), 10 (cirrus) and 11 (snow/ice)**. SCL is delivered at 20 m ([Sentinel Hub S2 L2A](https://docs.sentinel-hub.com/api/latest/data/sentinel-2-l2a/); [SCL script](https://custom-scripts.sentinel-hub.com/sentinel-2/scene-classification)). Compute **per-field cloud cover per date** as masked pixels divided by pixels in the polygon. Hide dates above roughly 20–30% masked from the timeline, and require 90–95% clear pixels for zones and anomalies. These thresholds are engineering defaults. SCL under-detects thin cloud and shadow edges, so a one-pixel dilation of the mask is cheap insurance.

The trap is ESA's **processing baseline 04.00 (January 2022)**, which added a −1000 offset to L2A digital numbers ([Sentinel Hub S2 L2A](https://docs.sentinel-hub.com/api/latest/data/sentinel-2-l2a/)). NDVI is not offset-invariant. Skipping the correction inflates NIR + Red and pulls NDVI toward zero, and the error is worst over bare soil. Community evidence says Earth Search applied the offset to its `sentinel-2-l2a` items, although the `boa_offset_applied` tag is sometimes wrong ([eoreader discussion #120](https://www.github.com/sertit/eoreader/discussions/120)). For `sentinel-2-c1-l2a`, the expected convention, which still needs checking against one item, is that DNs keep the offset and each asset declares `raster:bands` `scale: 0.0001, offset: -0.1`. The robust rule is to compute reflectance as `DN*scale + offset` from the asset metadata. If that metadata is missing, apply `(DN − 1000)/10000` when the baseline is ≥ 04.00 and the offset is not marked as applied. CDSE sidesteps the issue with `harmonizeValues`, which is on by default ([Sentinel Hub S2 L2A](https://docs.sentinel-hub.com/api/latest/data/sentinel-2-l2a/)).

### Fallbacks: CDSE for quality, Planetary Computer for Landsat, EOS as a paid option

| Source | Key/auth | Cloud masking | Browser-direct? | Limits | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Earth Search v1 + S3 COGs | None | Client-side SCL | Yes, if CORS confirmed | Undocumented | **Primary** (Sentinel-2) |
| CDSE Sentinel Hub (Statistical, Process, Catalog APIs) | Free account, OAuth client credentials | Server-side via evalscript `dataMask` | Possible as SPA client, but secret exposure risk; **proxy via NestJS** | 10,000 PU/month, 300 req/min | **Fallback / higher quality time series** |
| Microsoft Planetary Computer (STAC, SAS, Data API) | None (SAS token from `/sas`) | Client-side, or expression in Data API | Likely; Azure Blob CORS unverified | Undocumented; `/sas` returns 429 | **Landsat 8/9**, second fallback |
| EOS API Connect (Statistics API) | API key in URL | `cloud_masking_level` 1–4, `exclude_cover_pixels` | No: key would leak | 10 req/min, async | Optional premium, server-side only |

CDSE gives every free account **10,000 processing units and 10,000 requests a month, at up to 300 per minute** ([CDSE Quotas](https://documentation.dataspace.copernicus.eu/Quotas.html)). Tokens come from the CDSE identity endpoint by client credentials and must be reused, because requesting them too often returns 429. A client can be registered as a single-page app only after the developer accepts a **"risk of exposure"** warning ([CDSE Authentication](https://documentation.dataspace.copernicus.eu/APIs/SentinelHub/Overview/Authentication.html)). A NestJS endpoint that caches the token and forwards Statistical and Process calls is therefore the right design, and its memory cost on Render is trivial. One Statistical API call with an NDVI evalscript, an SCL-based `dataMask` and a `P1D` interval returns a whole season's per-date mean, standard deviation and percentiles, plus sample and no-data counts that give per-field cloud cover. A 100 ha field should cost on the order of one PU per date, which should be checked with the PU calculator ([CDSE PU](https://documentation.dataspace.copernicus.eu/APIs/SentinelHub/Overview/ProcessingUnit.html)). Use `sh.dataspace.copernicus.eu`, not `services.sentinel-hub.com`, which needs a paid Planet account. Two open risks remain. The quota page is silent on commercial use, so read the CDSE terms before launch ([CDSE Quotas](https://documentation.dataspace.copernicus.eu/Quotas.html)). And Render free-tier cold starts add an estimated 30–60 s to the first proxied call.

Planetary Computer retired its JupyterHub in June 2024 but kept its data and APIs **"available and unchanged" and usable without an account**. The `/sas` endpoint returns 429 when its unstated limit is exceeded ([PC discussion #347](https://github.com/microsoft/PlanetaryComputer/discussions/347)). Its TiTiler-based Data API can render item and mosaic tiles server-side ([PC Data API notebook](https://nbviewer.org/github/microsoft/PlanetaryComputerExamples/blob/conus404/quickstarts/using-the-data-api.ipynb)). Its service runs under preview terms ([PC terms](https://planetarycomputer.microsoft.com/terms)), so it is a fallback rather than a foundation. Its real value to AgroCloud is `landsat-c2-l2`, which is reachable without AWS credentials. The EOS Statistics API returns per-scene min/max/mean/median/p10/p90 with cloud filtering. However, it is asynchronous (create a task, then poll), capped at **10 requests per minute per key**, and its docs give no free tier ([EOS Statistics API](https://doc.eos.com/docs/statistics/)), so keep it behind the backend as an optional paid upgrade.

Latency is estimated, not measured: about 0.3–1.5 s for an Earth Search STAC query and 1–4 s per date on the COG path from us-west-2, more from the Middle East or Europe because of round-trip time. The smoke test settles the open questions in ten minutes. From the GitHub Pages origin, POST to Earth Search `/search`, then fetch `item.assets.red.href` with `Range: bytes=0-16383` and expect a `206` response with `Access-Control-Allow-Origin`. Repeat for a SAS-signed Planetary Computer blob and the CDSE statistics endpoint.

## Productivity zones need multi-season NDVI, three classes and an equipment-sized minimum

Commercial zoning uses NDVI from many seasons. OneSoil looks back **up to ten seasons**, picks scenes at the growth stage where NDVI best predicts yield, and drops seasons affected by flooding, weeds, disease or boundary changes (users can edit the list). It outputs **three zones expressed as relative yield deviation** (e.g., +13%, 0, −14%) and labels each zone stable or unstable. Its model was trained on about 40,000 ha of NDVI-to-harvest data from 2014–2018 ([OneSoil](https://onesoil.ai/en/resources/what-are-productivity-zones)). EOS builds seeding and P&K zones from all cloud-free images in a user-selected period back to 2016, excludes anomalies and advises leaving out an unharvested current year. It defaults to **3 zones within a 2–7 range**. Nitrogen maps use a single recent image, and Map Builder blends up to five weighted layers such as NDVI and elevation ([EOS Zoning](https://eos.com/user-guide/crop-monitoring/zoning)). Extension guidance agrees: **3–6 zones**, which need not be contiguous ([UNL EC154](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/ec154/2009/pdf/view/ec154-2009.pdf)). Start with three, and **make zones wider than the application equipment** ([UMD FS-1046](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/precision-soil-sampling-helps-farmers-target-nutrient-application-fs-1046)). No vendor discloses its classifier, smoothing or minimum polygon size. The best documented academic method z-scores NDVI, applies fuzzy c-means across 2–6 zones, and chooses the count that minimises the FPI and NCE indices. Its NDVI zones matched 7-year yield zones at up to **95% pixel agreement** ([Ali et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/12/2/231/html)).

The recommended pipeline follows from those sources, with the specific numbers as tunable defaults:

1. For each of the last 3–10 seasons, take the maximum-NDVI clear scene inside a crop-specific window: around heading for cereals, around VT–R1 for maize. This uses the sowing date AgroCloud already stores per crop.
2. Normalise each date to relative NDVI, `NDVI / field mean`. This preserves OneSoil-style "+13%" semantics. Use z-scores if contrast differs a lot between years.
3. Drop any season whose map correlates poorly (r < ~0.3) with the mean of the others.
4. Average per pixel and keep the coefficient of variation across years as a stability flag.
5. Classify by 1-D k-means, quantiles or fixed bands (<0.9 / 0.9–1.1 / >1.1).

A 500 ha field is only about 50,000 pixels, so this runs in well under a second in JS. Smooth with a 3×3 or 5×5 majority filter, then merge connected regions smaller than a minimum mapping unit of roughly 0.25–0.5 ha (25–50 pixels), or more for wide booms. Polygonise with d3-contour or PostGIS raster, then simplify, make valid and clip to the boundary. For Middle Eastern centre pivots, clip to the pivot circle and expect ring and wheel-track artefacts that call for a larger filter. That advice is an engineering inference with no source behind it.

VRA rates come from per-zone inputs. EOS takes a rate per zone and compares the result with a flat rate in a savings calculator. It exports SHP presets for John Deere, Amazone and Trimble plus **ISO-XML for ISOBUS** ([EOS Zoning](https://eos.com/user-guide/crop-monitoring/zoning)). OneSoil sets zone rates as a percentage deviation from a standard rate and recommends three zones because they simplify control strips ([OneSoil VRA help](https://help.onesoil.ai/en/articles/6115810-how-to-create-a-vra-map-for-planting-fertilization-or-applying-plant-protection-products)). WinField's "direct" example steps corn seeding from 25,000 to 32,000 plants/ac and N from 150 to 210 lb/ac across zones, against a flat 185, and places check strips across all zones ([WinField United](https://www.winfieldunited.com/news-and-insights/prescription-basics-build-a-variable-rate-plan)). In-season N can be direct (more where potential is high) or compensatory (more where it is low), so leave that choice to the user explicitly. For export, a zipped shapefile needs `.shp/.dbf/.shx/.prj` with one base name at the ZIP root, and **DBF field names are limited to 10 characters** ([Leaf file preparation](https://docs.withleaf.io/guides/planning/file-preparation)). ISO-XML treatment zones must use the official DDI units. Setpoint mass is **DDI 6 in mg/m²**, so 150 kg/ha is written as 15,000. Setpoint volume is DDI 1 in mm³/m² and setpoint count is DDI 11 in /m². DDI 7 is the *actual* rate, not the setpoint ([isobus.net DDI list](https://www.isobus.net/isobus/dDEntity)). One widely indexed tutorial gets this wrong ([ag-gis tutorial](https://ag-gis.pages.dev/yield-mapping-variable-rate-prescription-generation/variable-rate-export-to-isoxml/)), so take DDI values only from the official registry.

## Soil grids, scouting and anomaly alerts turn maps into field work

Grid sampling for P, K and pH should use **one sample per 1 acre for best accuracy and never coarser than one per 2.5 acres** (about 0.4–1 ha). Take about five cores within 8–10 ft of each point to 8 in depth, on an **offset grid**, which gives more information per dollar. Zone sampling instead composites **10–15 cores per zone** ([UNL EC154](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/ec154/2009/pdf/view/ec154-2009.pdf)). Do not align grids with old boundaries or ditches. P and K maps stay useful for about five years and pH maps for about ten ([UMD FS-1046](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/precision-soil-sampling-helps-farmers-target-nutrient-application-fs-1046)). In PostGIS, generate cells with `ST_SquareGrid` or `ST_HexagonGrid`, offset alternate rows by half a cell, and drop points within about 20 m of the boundary using `ST_Buffer(boundary, -20)`. For zone sampling, place one point per zone at `ST_PointOnSurface` of an inward-buffered zone. Give samples stable IDs such as `F012-2026-G07`, export GPX, KML and CSV, and import lab CSVs by matching sample ID. For interpolation, be cautious with the familiar default: IDW with power 2 was the method **most sensitive to single outlier samples** in an Ohio comparison, and kriging was excluded there because the data failed stationarity ([Matcham et al. 2021](https://soilfertility.osu.edu/sites/soilf/files/imce/Publications/Matcham%20et%20al.%20-%202021%20-%20Implications%20of%20choosing%20different%20interpolation%20m.pdf)). Ship IDW with leave-one-out RMSE shown to the user, offer kriging only on well-sampled fields, then bin rates and polygonise them as for zones. The soil-test-to-rate curves (sufficiency vs build-up/maintenance, Olsen P for calcareous soils) are regional, were not researched here, and should be pluggable.

Vendors document scouting only at a marketing level. FieldView frames it as spotting "dark green versus light green" inconsistencies from imagery ([FieldView blog](https://climate.com/blog/scouting-from-above)), and OneSoil and Cropwise attach geotagged notes, photos and offline use ([OneSoil help](https://help.onesoil.ai/en/articles/5237584-how-the-onesoil-web-and-mobile-apps-differ); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1547493499)). A sensible model has two tables:

- **`scouting_task`**: Point or Polygon geometry, `source` (manual / ndvi_anomaly / zone / sample_point), issue type and severity, assignee, status and due date.
- **`scouting_observation`**: GPS fix with accuracy, findings JSON and photo keys.

Completed tasks should write to the existing activity log. Field navigation uses `watchPosition` with distance and bearing. Offline use comes from a PWA with cached NDVI overlays and an IndexedDB edit queue. Add irrigation-specific issue types for pivots, such as nozzle blockage, end-gun failure and salinity.

Anomaly detection has **no published industry-standard ΔNDVI threshold**, and no vendor discloses its internals. The literature favours field-relative thresholds. One Sentinel-2 study set dynamic thresholds per field and growth stage by trimming histogram tails, and found that **false anomalies cluster at field edges** ([Aberystwyth](https://research.aber.ac.uk/en/publications/a-novel-approach-for-monitoring-in-field-crop-anomalies-a-case-st/)). Taranis uses a field-relative "dynamic" NDVI display for the same reason ([Taranis KB](https://knowledge.taranis.ag/portal/en/kb/articles/get-more-from-ndvi-how-standard-and-dynamic-views-help-you-act-faster)). A practical default:

1. Take two dates that are both at least 90–95% clear inside the field, and keep only pixels valid in both.
2. Subtract the field-median change, so phenology and atmosphere cancel out.
3. Flag residuals below −0.10 (moderate) or −0.15 (strong), or below median − 2σ using a MAD-based σ.
4. Drop a 10–20 m inner edge.
5. Open morphologically, then keep 8-connected clusters of at least 0.1–0.25 ha.
6. Polygonise each cluster and suggest a scouting task at its `ST_PointOnSurface`.
7. Alert only if the anomalous area exceeds a field share or hectare threshold, and re-alert only if it grows.
8. Suppress alerts when the whole-field mean falls by more than ~0.1 (senescence or harvest).

Consider NDRE for dense late canopies, where NDVI saturates. All these numbers are starting points to tune on local fields.

## Conclusion

The research changes AgroCloud's priorities more than its feature list. The original mockup already matched EOS's module set. What the market now rewards is turning imagery into "go here next" (anomaly-ranked scouting, weekly digests, field-grounded AI) rather than more map types. A leaderboard and Map Builder are cheap once per-field statistics exist, but they should come after the timeline, anomaly and scouting loop that makes users return weekly. Architecturally, the static-front-end constraint helps rather than hurts. Keyless STAC plus COG range reads put the per-pixel work in the user's browser, so the Render free tier only needs to cache statistics, hold the CDSE token and store zones, tasks and samples in PostGIS. That keeps cost near zero and removes the backend's sleep from the interactive path.

The main risks are not algorithmic. Unverified CORS behaviour, the Earth Search v2 collection rename, the Collection 1 scale/offset convention, CDSE's silence on commercial use, and Planetary Computer's preview terms each threaten a hard dependency. A smoke test, configurable collection IDs, metadata-driven reflectance scaling and a pluggable source layer (Earth Search, then CDSE, then Planetary Computer) contain all of them. The agronomic defaults for zones, minimum mapping unit, sampling interpolation and ΔNDVI thresholds are well grounded in extension and academic work but not in vendor disclosures. AgroCloud should expose them as editable settings and check them against local yield or scouting results rather than hard-code them.
